Role of Closest Hit Shaders in GLSL Ray Tracing

In GLSL ray tracing pipelines, the closest hit shader (.rchit) is the primary stage responsible for calculating the visual appearance of surfaces where a ray intersects geometry. This article provides a focused overview of how the closest hit shader executes within the ray tracing pipeline, processes geometric and material attributes, calculates lighting through bidirectional reflectance distribution functions (BRDFs), and casts secondary rays to achieve accurate global illumination and material realism.

Execution Trigger and Pipeline Context

The closest hit shader is invoked only after the ray traversal hardware confirms that a ray has intersected a primitive and that this intersection is the closest valid hit along the ray's trajectory (bounded by \(t_{\min}\) and \(t_{\max}\)). Unlike rasterization fragment shaders that execute per-pixel or per-sample across rasterized fragments, the closest hit shader operates dynamically per-ray, responding to both primary camera rays and secondary bounces (such as reflection, refraction, and diffuse indirect paths).

Accessing Intersection and Vertex Data

Upon invocation, the shader receives intersection attributes—most commonly hit coordinates like the barycentric coordinates of the hit triangle—via built-in input variables such as gl_HitAttributeEXT. The shader uses these coordinates to interpolate per-vertex attributes across the primitive:

  • Surface Normals and Tangents: Interpolated to determine the true orientation of the surface and facilitate normal or bump mapping.
  • Texture Coordinates (UVs): Interpolated to query material textures, including base color (albedo), roughness, metallic maps, and emission profiles.
  • Primitive and Instance IDs: Read via gl_PrimitiveID and gl_InstanceCustomIndexEXT to look up relevant buffer data, material indices, and transform matrices for multi-material scenes.

Evaluating Material Physics (BRDF/BSDF)

Once the material properties and geometric vectors (normal \(N\), view vector \(V\), and light vectors \(L\)) are gathered, the closest hit shader evaluates the material’s reflection model:

  1. Direct Lighting: The shader iterates over scene light sources, calculating direct specular and diffuse contributions based on models like Cook-Torrance microfacet BRDFs. To verify visibility between the hit point and light sources, the shader casts shadow rays via the traceRayEXT() function using an associated miss shader to detect occluders.
  2. Material Characteristics: Roughness determines specular dispersion, metalness controls the balance between dielectric diffuse reflection and conductive specular reflection, and transmission values govern refraction.
  3. Indirect Lighting and Recursion: For complex materials such as transparent glass, glossy metals, or rough surfaces requiring global illumination, the closest hit shader generates secondary rays. It samples directions based on importance sampling (for path tracing) or exact reflection/refraction vectors (via Snell's Law and Fresnel equations), calling traceRayEXT() recursively or iteratively.

Communicating via Ray Payloads

The primary mechanism for outputting data from a closest hit shader is the ray payload, declared with the rayPayloadInEXT storage qualifier. Rather than writing to a traditional framebuffer, the shader populates this payload structure with data such as accumulated radiance, hit distance, surface normal, or attenuation factors. This payload is passed back down the call chain to the ray generation shader or an earlier closest hit shader, allowing the renderer to compose the final pixel color through progressive ray accumulation.
