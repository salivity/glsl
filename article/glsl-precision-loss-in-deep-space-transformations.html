GLSL Precision Loss in Deep-Space Transformations

Rendering vast astronomical environments in real-time graphics engines frequently causes visual artifacts such as vertex jitter, geometry tearing, and severe Z-fighting. This article examines the technical causes of floating-point precision loss during deep-space camera transformations in GLSL. It covers how the limitations of 32-bit IEEE 754 floating-point representations, large coordinate ranges, catastrophic cancellation in matrix multiplications, and non-linear depth buffer distribution combine to degrade rendering quality at cosmic scales.

The Limits of IEEE 754 Single-Precision Floats

Standard GLSL shaders typically rely on 32-bit floating-point numbers (float or highp float). An IEEE 754 single-precision float uses:

  • 1 sign bit
  • 8 exponent bits
  • 23 mantissa (significand) bits

This layout delivers approximately 6 to 9 significant decimal digits of precision. In deep-space environments, coordinates often span millions or billions of meters (or kilometers). Because floating-point numbers distribute precision relative to their magnitude, the absolute distance between representable numbers increases as coordinates grow larger. For example, at a distance of \(10^7\) units from the origin, the spacing between consecutive representable numbers is roughly 1 unit. Any movement, animation, or vertex offset smaller than that spacing is rounded off, leading to distinct stair-stepping and vertex jittering.

Catastrophic Cancellation During Matrix Transformations

Camera transformations commonly compute local-to-clip coordinates using a unified Model-View-Projection (MVP) matrix:

\[\text{Clip Position} = P \times V \times M \times \text{Position}_{\text{local}}\]

In standard pipelines, the View matrix (\(V\)) translates the scene by the negative of the camera position. When both the camera and the rendered object are located far from the world origin, the GPU must subtract two extraordinarily large floating-point numbers to calculate a relatively small camera-relative distance:

\[\mathbf{p}_{\text{eye}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{world}} - \mathbf{c}_{\text{world}}\]

Because the low-order bits were discarded when the original large world coordinates were stored, the subtraction results in catastrophic cancellation. The remaining value loses significant digits, causing the geometry to distort, wobble, and tear relative to the camera frame.

Dynamic Range Mismatch in the Depth Buffer

Deep-space simulations require an extreme ratio between the near clipping plane (\(z_{near}\)) and the far clipping plane (\(z_{far}\)). Standard perspective projection matrices map view-space depth to Normalized Device Coordinates (NDC) non-linearly:

\[z_{ndc} = \frac{f + n}{f - n} + \frac{2fn}{f - n} \cdot \frac{1}{z_{eye}}\]

Most of the available floating-point depth precision is concentrated near \(z_{near}\). As the distance between \(z_{near}\) and \(z_{far}\) spans orders of magnitude (e.g., from 1 meter to \(10^{11}\) meters for celestial bodies), the depth buffer rapidly runs out of distinct bit patterns for distant objects. Multiple polygons occupying different physical positions map to the exact same depth value in the hardware depth buffer, resulting in persistent Z-fighting.

Interpolation Inaccuracies in the Rasterizer

Precision loss is not confined to the vertex shader. When world-space or view-space positions are passed as varying (or out) vectors to the fragment shader, the GPU's fixed-function rasterizer interpolates these values across triangle faces. When a triangle spans a vast coordinate range or is positioned far from the coordinate origin, the barycentric interpolation operates on imprecise 32-bit vertex values. This introduces micro-deviations across the primitive surface, which impairs lighting calculations, normal mapping, and atmospheric scattering algorithms that rely on smooth world-space positions.
